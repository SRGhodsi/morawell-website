When Responsibility Outpaces Trust

Can workplace simulations reveal what résumés cannot?

By MoraWell

LinkedIn WhatsApp

A Persian saying describes someone who hears only the letter “F” and is already on the way to Farahzad. It captures a familiar experience: when people understand one another well, a few words can convey what would otherwise require a lengthy explanation.

In traditional apprenticeships and family businesses, this understanding often developed over years. People learned not only their profession but also the habits, priorities and judgments of those around them. Experience and trust grew through shared work.

Today's professional world moves differently. People frequently change organizations, bringing valuable skills and impressive résumés but little shared history with their new colleagues.

An organization can hire someone's experience, but it cannot hire a shared history that does not yet exist.

Seven Perspectives on Experience, Trust and Decision-Making

1. Experience Travels More Easily Than Understanding

Professional knowledge can move from one organization to another. Understanding its people, unwritten rules, priorities and decision-making habits takes time to develop.

2. Trust Is Built Through Shared Experience

Credentials and recommendations offer evidence of past performance. Working together reveals something different: how people respond to uncertainty, handle disagreements and accept responsibility when decisions become difficult.

3. Familiarity Makes Communication Faster

Experienced colleagues often anticipate one another's concerns and intentions. Fewer explanations are needed, misunderstandings are reduced, and decisions can move faster. This does not require identical opinions, only a reliable understanding of how others think.

4. Middle Management Feels the Pressure

Middle managers must coordinate competing priorities while making timely decisions. Without sufficient mutual understanding, routine decisions may require repeated explanations, additional approvals or unnecessary escalation to senior management.

5. Every New Member Changes the Pattern

In physics, overlapping waves can reinforce or weaken one another. As an analogy, a new colleague entering an established team may strengthen its performance, disturb its coordination or create an entirely new pattern of interaction. Individual competence alone does not determine the outcome.

6. Agreement Is Not the Same as Effectiveness

Familiarity can improve coordination, but excessive agreement may discourage new ideas. A newcomer who challenges established assumptions can be valuable. The objective is not to recruit people who think alike, but to create teams capable of making better decisions together.

7. Simulation Can Reveal What a Résumé Cannot

Workplace simulations offer a possible way to observe candidates in realistic situations before assigning critical responsibilities. By introducing competing priorities, incomplete information and time pressure, organizations can examine not only individual decisions but also their effects on team coordination.

Simulations cannot replace the trust built through real experience or guarantee future performance. They can, however, reveal patterns of interaction that conventional recruitment methods may overlook.

The challenge for modern organizations is not to recreate the slow career paths of the past, but to develop better ways of understanding people before placing significant responsibility in their hands.

Perhaps the future of recruitment lies not only in examining what individuals have achieved, but also in exploring what happens when they become part of a team.
